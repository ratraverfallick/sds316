DV Prosecution Research Plan

Author

Catherine Xu

Phase 1 Research Questions

  1. Is there a relationship between a defendant’s racial group and the rate of arrests for domestic violence?
  2. For interracial cases of domestic violence, how does the race of the abuser and/or the victim moderate police response?
  3. Does gender play a differential role based on the race of the abuser specifically?

Personal Motivation

I am researching this topic because I interned with a family lawyer last summer and became interested in several aspects of family law, but particularly in the court proceedings for cases involving domestic violence. Although my lawyer’s work was mainly within civil courts (divorce and child custody), I wanted to specifically examine the role of race in criminal courts and identify whether or not/how instances of intimate partner violence are racialized in legal proceedings, as well as the role racial discrimination plays in interpreting the law regarding what constitutes a criminal act of domestic violence.

Broader Relevance

Given the US criminal legal system’s well-documented precedent for allowing racial bias to justify certain arrests and determine a particular defendant’s sentencing outcomes, I thought it would be really informative to see how that bias plays out when it comes to domestic and, more broadly, gender-based violence. When we consider how race affects perceptions of victimhood similarly to gender for any given crime, but especially domestic violence, I also thought it would be important to add a dimension to my research that explicitly investigates whether or not the race and gender of the abuser and the victim moderate the corresponding police/court response.

Example Projects

Race and Ethnicity Map of New York: I wanted to potentially create a spatial visualization (interactive map) of the police presence or surveillance cameras by neighborhood/region of Manhattan. To make it a trivariate map I’m hoping to find a way to layer the racial demographics of each neighborhood on top of the measure of surveillance. I think this visualization method will most effectively communicate the relationship between my variables.

Dismantle NYC’s Surveillance State: This project is a report and petition for Mayor Mamdani to act on his initiatives to dismantle the various forms of harmful government surveillance in NYC. It includes a lot of helpful background and data points, including the estimated costs of the specified reforms, which I will use to contextualize and broaden my coverage and discussion of the types of racially discriminatory surveillance technologies in NYC policing.

Vital City: Who’s the Guard Really Guarding in US Cities?: I liked how this project highlighted the discrepancy between the cities that Trump’s executive orders involving police force/action targeted and the actual prevalence of crime and violence in those cities. I want to build a similar narrative with my visualizations that highlights the areas of Manhattan that are most heavily surveilled/policed and compare it with the actual rate of violent crime that exists in those areas, with the idea that surveillance technologies are largely based on databases that are far from neutral, but based on arrest data that discriminates against certain racial groups.

Relevant Literature

Build a ten item literature review for work related to your questions.

As you consider which 10 to include, try to satisfy these criteria:

  • 1 - 2 written by someone on the inside or system impacted
  • 3ish non-academic sources
  • at least 5 academic sources
  • no more than 2 items that we’ve discussed in class / from the syllabus

No need to include a full citation. Instead, list them:

In the Image of Injustice: A Critical Examination of AI, Surveillance, and Systemic Racism in the United States: This article examines the detrimental impact of AI and its associated technologies in the conducting of crime risk assessments and justifications of surveillance. It covers how these technologies have significant error rates for people of color and are based on algorithms that resulted from a history of racial discrimination and over-policing in communities that are predominantly made up of people of color, thereby challenging the notion that AI and surveillance technologies are neutral/unbiased tools of justice.

Types of AI Surveillance: This article is one of the more “neutral” sources that I am planning to use mostly for background and context about the different types of AI-driven surveillance technologies that governments of various levels and nationalities use. I think this article is a good source of foundational knowledge about the predominant methods of government surveillance and also provides brief discussions of their potential shortcomings/negative impacts, particularly in cases of political fugitives and/or dissidents.

SURVEILLANCE OF BLACK LIVES AS INJURY-IN-FACT: This article is a legal review of the historical precedent for surveilling Black bodies and communities, as well as the legal parameters and justifications for social media surveillance in the digital era. It provides the legal basis for government surveillance measures and the history of racialized surveillance that has evolved into the disproportionate surveillance and targeted policing of Black people’s social media activity.

Fragile Algorithms and Fallible Decision-Makers: Lessons from the Justice System: The authors delve into the various inconsistencies and biases that artificial intelligence predictive algorithms display when used in the criminal justice system. Beyond issues with training the algorithms and addressing conflicts between machine-human assessments, they argue that using it to make important justice decisions like pre-trial release proves to be highly problematic, especially where Black defendants are concerned. I am also interested in this article because it specifically works with data from New York City.

The Dynamics of Racial Residential Segregation: This is a broad historical review of various trends in residential segregation of people of color, particularly Black people, in major American cities and their metropolitan areas. It provides a thorough summary and theorization of the historical development of residential segregation, one of many theories being that it is entrenched by generational poverty and oppressive racialized systems/structures that contribute to stark racial disparities in urban geography. I am interested in using this article as a theoretical basis for my research question as it deals explicitly with racial residential segregation in urban areas.

ICE has spun a massive surveillance web. We talked to people caught in it: NPR covers the widespread use of mass surveillance technologies in ICE raids and stops/detentions, particularly APLR, facial recognition, and social media (Meta). The article is based on interviews with activists/observers and immigrants who were directly detained or stopped by ICE to illuminate the lived ways in which surveillance tools enable ICE and the Trump administration conduct their crackdowns. I will use this work to gain a better understanding of justice-impacted individuals’ encounters with mass surveillance technologies.

Opinion: How Exclusionary Zoning Perpetuates Segregation in New York: This op-ed argues that the history of redlining in New York City has persisted till the modern day as a result of exclusionary zoning, making the city and its metro area the third most segregated region of the country. Calling out the frequently deployed arguments, both historical and modern, about how people of color are a detriment to and unwelcome intrusion on the local economy, the article argues that the city is zoned deliberately to cater to a wealthy, white populace, exacerbated by and directly contributing to gentrification and sky-high housing costs.

Why New York’s ‘Precision Policing’ Raises Civil Rights Concerns: The Marshall Project addresses the use of ‘precision policing’ by the NYPD and the deployment of more police to areas that are deemed to be more high-crime. For example, proponents at the NYPD claim this technology has significantly reduced crime in The Bronx, an argument that contains a notable layer of bias as the Bronx has historically been populated by predominantly Black and brown communities. The article addresses the various civil rights implications attached to these ‘neutral’ surveillance measures that justify increaed police presence and abuses of power in historically marginalized neighborhoods.

Analyzing the First Year of NYC Police Encounter Data from the How Many Stops Act: This source is a brief by the Legal Defense Fund that uses data collected from the How Many Stops Act to highlight the disproportionate policing of Black people in NYC based on the average number of police stops and interactions per person as a function of their race. Additionally, it covers the geographical correlation based on the disproportionate number of encounters with police based on whether or not a given precinct has more Black residents. It argues for more data transparency so that citizens and policymakers are aware of how taxpayer money is put towards perpetuating these racially discriminatory systems.

[Predict and Surveil: Data, Discretion, and the Future of Policing - Ch. 3]: I wanted to include this chapter as part of my relevant literature because it includes the author’s interactions with people who work at Palantir. I found it very illuminative of the general attitude of people who partake in and support mass surveillance of criminal suspects and political dissidents. It also delves into how the technology is used pretty indiscriminately, without much regard for the rights of private citizens and whether or not they are actually culpable for a given crime.
